# Product

<!-- impeccable:product-schema 1 -->

## Platform

web

## Users

One author and a small, known audience. **Danny** keeps the Atlas — he is a non-coder
who works with an AI collaborator, adds hikes through a guided wizard, and reads his own
record more than anyone. **Friends and family** are the visitors: people who know him,
often people who appear in the record by name, arriving on a desktop browser from a link
he sent. Nobody arrives via search, nobody is being sold anything, and nobody needs
onboarding into what a hike is.

The visitor's job is not a task. It is to look at where somebody has been.

## Product Purpose

The Trailprint Atlas is a living journal of Danny's hiking life, told through maps, data
and (in a later phase) his own writing. It exists so that 123 outings — GPX tracks,
photographs, elevations, companions, weather — read as one continuous life outdoors
rather than a list of activities.

Success is that it feels like *his* atlas: a bound volume somebody made, not an app
somebody installed. It is never "finished" — it grows for as long as he keeps hiking.

## Positioning

**Deliberately not a generic hiking app.** Strava, AllTrails and Gaia all show the same
outing the same way, because they must work for everyone. The Atlas shows one person's
ground, so every choice can be specific to it: hikes are collected state by state at home
and country by country abroad; a summit records the highest ground actually *walked*, not
the peak's published height; a trip is a chapter with a curated opening photograph; a
companion has a service record rather than a row in a table.

The mechanism a neighbouring product could not copy is that the dataset is one person's
and complete, so the design can make claims about it — "one outing in four was walked
with Will R." — that a general tool can never make.

## Operating Context

- **Desktop browsers.** Mobile is deliberately deferred to its own future phase. Mobile
  findings are out of scope and should not be raised.
- **The site is read in one sitting**, usually after Danny sends a link, often by someone
  looking for themselves or a trip they were on.
- **Development** is Live Server against local files; Danny watches changes land in the
  browser and approves each step before the next. He develops in Zen (Gecko); automated
  checks drive Chrome (Blink). Cross-engine parity is a known, scheduled debt.
- **Deployment** is a push to `main`; GitHub Pages redeploys immediately. There is no
  staging environment, so the site must work at every commit.

## Capabilities and Constraints

**These are hard. Work that violates them cannot ship, however good it looks.**

- **No build step, no bundler, no framework, no npm.** Static HTML + CSS + vanilla ES6.
  Libraries are pinned from a CDN in each page's own HTML (Leaflet 1.9.4, MapLibre on the
  home page only). This is an audited architectural choice, not an accident or a
  limitation to route around — renovate it, don't replace it.
- **$0/month, permanently.** GitHub Pages, Cloudinary's free tier for every photograph,
  Open-Meteo for historical weather. Anything with a bill is out.
- **`data/hikes.json` is the single source of truth.** Never hardcode hike data anywhere
  else; a feature needing new per-hike information adds a field to the schema first.
  Several other data files are generated by tools in `tools/` and must not be hand-edited.
- **No inline CSS** anywhere except `404.html`, which is self-contained by design.
- **A page must not gain another bare `100vh` fixed shell** without a `min-height` floor.
  Eight of nine stylesheets already have one and none has a floor; that is a scheduled
  repair, and new work must not enlarge it.
- The full technical record — file map, schema, naming, and the reasoning behind every
  settled decision — lives in `CLAUDE.md`. **Read it before proposing changes**; a large
  number of the obvious suggestions have already been tried, measured and rejected there,
  with the measurements written down.

## Brand Commitments

- **The voice rule.** The `notes` field, The Overlook's personal statement, and the
  loading phrases are **Danny's words only**. Never draft, invent or "improve" them. The
  `description`/`flora`/`fauna` fields are an AI-drafted field-guide layer and may be
  written, subject to his review. Descriptions run ~60–70 words, two or three tight
  sentences.
- **No emoji, ever, as interface icons.** They read as cheap and machine-made. Icons are
  hand-drawn SVG or they are placeholder slots until one is drawn.
- **The material is paper.** Parchment, engraved rules and collars, brass, ink that
  carries a year's colour, and the language of survey and cartography (plates, courses,
  leaves, collars, stations, traverses). The palette is earthy and low-contrast on
  purpose; it is the identity, not an oversight.
- **Named recurring components exist and are shared**, notably the `.atlas-door` — one
  brass card behind *every* crossing between pages. Never restyle one door alone.
- **Em-dashes used as mid-sentence asides read as AI writing** and are avoided in prose.

## Evidence on Hand

Everything in the Atlas is real and verifiable, which is the point.

- **123 hike records** with real dates, coordinates, mileage and companions.
- **Raw GPX tracks** per hike, archived; simplified geometry served to the map.
- **Real photographs** on Cloudinary, ~2,000+, hand-filed per hike.
- **USGS 3DEP-corrected elevations** — GPS altitude reads 15–130 ft high, so every
  profile and summit height was ground-truthed against the national elevation model and
  benchmarked to within 1–3 ft of published summits.
- **Baked historical weather** per hike from Open-Meteo, including the boots-on window.

**Absences that must never be filled by invention:** `notes` is `null` on every one of
the 123 records — that is Danny's journal and a later phase fills it, in his words. There
are no testimonials, no visitor numbers, no reviews, no awards. A photograph that does
not exist is not described as though it does.

## Product Principles

1. **Data as narrative.** Don't show a number; make it tell the story of a day outside.
2. **Visual first.** Map-centric and immersive. Where a page is about place, the land
   *is* the page and the instruments float on it.
3. **Craft over tooling.** Vanilla web technology, held to a very high standard. The
   constraint is the discipline, and every page must work at every commit.
4. **Personal and authentic.** The voice belongs to Danny. The Atlas records what
   actually happened, to the foot and to the minute, and says so plainly when it doesn't
   know.
5. **It is a volume, not a product.** No sign-ups, no settings, no dashboards, no
   marketing surfaces, no growth mechanics. Somebody made this by hand.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Confirmed with Danny, 8 August 2026. The bar is **keyboard reach and motion respect**:

- Everything that can be clicked should be **reachable and operable by keyboard**, and
  reaching it must not destroy what it was pointing at.
- **`prefers-reduced-motion` is honoured** wherever the Atlas moves — and it moves a lot
  (an opening film, page turns, drawing ink, veil cuts, revealing scrolls).
- Interactive elements carry **honest labels** for screen readers, sized to what a person
  actually needs rather than reading the whole panel aloud on every hover.

**KEYBOARD REACH IS A GOAL, NOT A VETO.** Danny's ruling, 8 August 2026: *a good feature
is never altered or removed merely because a keyboard cannot reach it.* Where a gesture
genuinely resists the keyboard — scrubbing a traverse, walking a timeline, a hover that
lights ink somewhere else — the right finding is "here is a way to also reach this", and
if there is no good way, the honest answer is to leave the feature alone and record the
gap. A finding that proposes deleting or watering down working behaviour to satisfy this
section is answering the wrong question.

The **interactive map is where this actually bites**, and it is explicitly deferred: it
is the page most built out of hover, scrub and pointer gestures, and it gets its own
larger conversation when its turn comes rather than being decided in passing here.

**Formal WCAG contrast compliance is explicitly not the bar.** The parchment palette and
its engraved greys are the Atlas's identity; findings that would darken them to hit a
ratio are declined by decision, not by oversight. Raise a contrast issue only where text
is genuinely hard to read for an ordinary reader.
